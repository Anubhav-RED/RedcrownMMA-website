How Many Calories Does
MMA Training Burn?

The real math, not the headline number.

Search this question and you'll get the same handful of numbers everywhere: 800 calories an hour, 1000 calories an hour, sometimes higher. Almost none of them tell you what those numbers actually assume.

They assume you're sparring, hard, for the entire hour. Almost no real class works that way, and pretending otherwise isn't a favour to anyone deciding whether to walk through the door.

The Actual Science, Briefly

Calorie burn during exercise is estimated using something called a MET value, metabolic equivalent of task, a measure of how many times harder an activity works your body compared to sitting still. Published research (the Compendium of Physical Activities, the standard reference researchers use) puts combat-sports training somewhere between MET 8 and MET 12 depending on intensity, with live sparring and grappling at the higher end and technical, stop-start drilling meaningfully lower.

The formula: calories burned per minute = (MET × 3.5 × your weight in kg) ÷ 200. Multiply by however many minutes you trained.

What an Actual Hour Looks Like

A real class isn't one continuous intensity from start to finish. A typical structure runs 15 minutes of warm-up, the bulk of the hour on technical work and drilling, and a 5 to 10 minute conditioning finisher, sometimes HIIT-style, depending on the coach and the day.

Run the honest math phase by phase for a 70kg adult, and a full hour lands around 450 to 550 calories, not 900. The warm-up and technical portions simply don't run at sparring intensity, and pretending they do is how the internet arrived at its inflated headline number in the first place.

That number moves. Classes with more live sparring or rolling built in push meaningfully higher. A pure conditioning-focused session can too. But 450 to 550 is the honest baseline for a normal technical-heavy class, and it's still a serious hour of work, several hundred calories more than sitting through the same hour at a desk.

"The only real data we've got is watching people breathe after class. That's not a joke, that's the whole readout."

Does the Discipline Change the Number?

Yes, and it lines up with the published research. Wrestling and BJJ, grappling-heavy training, tend to sit at the top of the range, the constant physical contact and positional battling keeps output high almost continuously. Muay Thai and kickboxing sit close behind and are a genuinely strong choice if fitness, not competition, is the actual goal, sustained striking output with less of grappling's stop-start scrambling. Boxing and general strength and conditioning work land in a similar middle range, depending heavily on how the specific session is run.

Does Unlimited Mat Time Mean Double the Burn?

Not proportionally, and it would be dishonest to claim otherwise. Staying an extra hour does add real total calories, but almost nobody sustains hour-one intensity into hour two. Most people shift toward lighter technical work or slower rolling once the tank starts running low, which is normal and fine, just worth knowing before you assume three hours equals three times the burn.

Beginners Feel More Than They Burn

This one surprises people. A first-week beginner usually finishes a class feeling completely wrecked, and genuinely believes they went as hard as anyone in the room. In terms of actual output, they often move slower than an experienced student working through the same drill, simply because pace and technique are still being built. High perceived effort, lower actual output, that's normal, it's not a sign of doing anything wrong, and it changes within weeks as movement gets more efficient.
