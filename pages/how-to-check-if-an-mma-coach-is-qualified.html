Is Your MMA Coach
Actually Qualified?

What you can verify, what to ask, and when to walk away.

In India, "coach" is not a licensed title. Anyone can print it on a banner, post a few clips and open a batch. That makes choosing an MMA gym harder than it should be, especially for a beginner who cannot yet tell good technique from loud technique.

This guide gives you a simple way to check any coach, at any gym, including ours. Everything below is something a stranger can verify.

What You Can Actually Verify

  • Belt lineage. BJJ promotions form a chain. Ask who awarded the belt, and who awarded that person's. Then check, by DM or call, with the person named. Real lineage survives one phone call.
  • Competition history. You do not need a full fight record. A coach can still name the event, the year and the result of a competition they entered. "National medalist" should come with an event you can look up.
  • Coaching history. How long have they taught, and where? Have their students competed? Years of teaching matter more than years of posting.
  • Ongoing training. The best coaches are still students. Ask who they train under now. A coach who stopped learning usually stops improving their students too.
  • How they teach. Watch a class. Does the coach explain, demonstrate and correct, or only count reps and shout? Do beginners get attention?

Questions to Ask Before You Join

  • Who promoted your belt, and who promoted them?
  • Which competitions have you entered, and when?
  • How long have you coached, and have your students competed?
  • Who do you train under now?
  • What happens to a beginner in the first month? Do I have to spar?
  • Can I watch a class, or try one first?

Red Flags

  • Titles with no source. "World champion" or "international fighter" with no event, year or result.
  • Belts with no named promoter. If the answer to "who gave you your belt?" is vague, treat the belt as unproven.
  • Pressure to pay first. Long plans pushed before you have seen a class.
  • No trial, no watching. A coach confident in their teaching has no reason to hide it.
  • Hard sparring for beginners. New students should build skill and fitness first, and never be thrown in to prove toughness.
  • Guarantees. No honest coach guarantees fat loss, fights or belts.
  • Attacking other gyms instead of showing their own work.

Credentials Are Not Everything

A medal proves someone can compete. It does not prove they can teach a nervous 28-year-old how to throw a jab. Our own order of priorities when choosing any gym: first, whether the coach suits you, because you will not keep showing up to someone you dislike; second, whether the credentials are real and not marketing language; third, whether the environment is genuinely beginner-friendly. Treat all three as the bare minimum, whatever the fee.
